THE PREMISEAn essay on human reinvention

We taught machines to think.
Now, what will we make
of ourselves?

For much of modern life, we have treated intelligence as a passport to usefulness. Schools cultivate it. Professions reward it. Institutions turn it into authority.

What happens when more of those capabilities can be reproduced? The change reaches beyond the workplace. It touches the letter we write, the choices we make, the recognition we seek, and the stories we tell about why a life matters.

This essay proposes four ages through which to examine that change. Each asks what happens to a different human claim: this is my voice; this is my choice; this is my contribution; this is who I am.

↳

Four lenses, not a timetable. These ages may overlap, unfold unevenly, or never arrive as imagined. The opening scenes are fiction. The argument is an invitation to debate, not a prediction of a single human future.

AGE ONE / AUTHENTICITYI

The Mirror

The crisis of authenticity

What is real when
our expressions can
be imitated?

NEXT: THE SURRENDER ↓

IMAGINE THIS

She asks a machine to help write a letter to her father. The words are warmer, clearer, braver than anything she has managed alone. He calls in tears. For the first time in years, he feels understood.

The feeling is real. She is less certain about the voice.

When expression stops being evidence

A letter carries more than information. It suggests that someone paused, struggled, remembered, and chose. When a system can produce the outward signs of those acts, the reader has less certainty about what happened behind the words.

That does not make the relationship false. A person who cannot easily find language may finally be able to speak. Translation, collaboration, and borrowed phrases have long been part of human expression. The question is what the sender means, what the recipient believes, and what each has agreed to share.

In a classroom, a polished essay may no longer reveal what a student understands. In a friendship, a perfect apology may leave its author unchanged. Institutions must decide which processes matter, rather than assuming that a convincing result proves the effort or understanding they meant to reward.

The mirror works in both directions

We may also begin composing ourselves for machines: smoothing out the unusual phrase, choosing the image likely to be recommended, presenting a life that fits a system’s categories. The risk is not only synthetic expression. It is the narrowing of human expression to what systems recognize.

Authenticity need not mean untouched by tools. It can mean taking responsibility for what we say, being honest about its making, and remaining answerable to the person receiving it. Cultures will draw those boundaries differently.

An alternative path. Tools could help more people find a voice while schools, artists, and communities make the process of creation more visible. Better imitation alone neither establishes machine experience nor empties human expression of meaning.

Authenticity means
ownership, honesty, and accountability.
AGE TWO / AGENCYII

The Surrender

The erosion of agency

What is freedom
when choosing
becomes optional?

NEXT: THE IRRELEVANCE ↓

IMAGINE THIS

His assistant rearranges meetings, negotiates bills, and finds a course that leads to a better job. He has more time and fewer regrets. One evening, he turns it off and tries to plan the next year.

Nothing prevents him from choosing. He no longer knows where to begin.

The quiet distance between choice and judgment

Delegation is ordinary. We rely on colleagues, tools, and institutions because a life lived entirely unaided would be smaller. An assistant that removes tedious work may make room for more consequential decisions.

But agency requires more than a button marked “approve.” We need enough understanding to question a recommendation, enough practice to form our own judgment, and enough authority to make a refusal stick. Convenience becomes dependence when those capacities disappear.

AI agents connect model outputs to tools that can act. A recommendation can become a booking, a purchase, or a change to a record. Permission boundaries and opportunities to review an action therefore become part of the design of everyday power. [2]

Whose preferences become the default?

A system that optimizes a life must optimize for something. The user’s stated wishes, an employer’s targets, and a platform’s commercial interests may point in different directions. If its assumptions are hard to see, even successful assistance can conceal a transfer of authority.

A survey of knowledge workers linked greater confidence in generative AI with less reported critical thinking. It also found that thinking shifted toward checking and integrating outputs. This is evidence about reported practice, not proof of permanent cognitive decline. [3]

An alternative path. Delegation can remain informed, limited, and reversible. People can retain skills, inspect the reasons for a decision, and appeal to someone empowered to change it. The institutional question is who gets those options—and who must accept the default.

A choice is not fully ours
if we cannot meaningfully refuse it.
AGE THREE / PURPOSEIII

The Irrelevance

The crisis of purpose

Who are we
when our skills
are no longer scarce?

NEXT: THE BECOMING ↓

IMAGINE THIS

For thirty years, people brought him problems no one else could solve. Now the office buys that expertise by the minute. The city is wealthier. His bills are covered. At dinner, someone asks what he does.

He has never found the question so difficult.

Unneeded for a task. Still needed as a person.

Work can provide wages, rhythm, companionship, and a claim to recognition. Losing a role may unsettle all four, even when income is protected. Yet they are different losses, and none establishes that a person has become worthless.

The title of this age names a fear, not a verdict on human value. A society can confuse market demand with moral worth. If a scarce skill becomes abundant, that confusion is exposed: did we respect the person, or only the price their ability could command?

Four questions must stay separate. Which tasks change? Who receives the gains? What happens to status? Where do people find belonging? The ILO’s 2025 exposure study points to job transformation as the more likely effect of generative AI, rather than wholesale replacement. Exposure measures potential; it is not a count of jobs already lost. [4]

Abundance for whom?

Less expensive cognitive work does not make ownership, energy, land, or political power equally available. A highly productive economy could still deny many people security. Reinvention becomes a hollow promise if only those who own the systems can afford it.

Nor does everyone begin with a professional identity at the center of life. Caregiving, family, craft, spiritual practice, and community obligation already organize meaning in many settings. The disruption will vary with institutions and culture; one displaced professional cannot stand in for humanity.

An alternative path. New forms of work may emerge, and existing roles may expand. Shared gains, shorter working time, and recognition for unpaid contribution could loosen the bond between dignity and employment. None of these arrangements follows automatically from technical capability.

If we no longer have to prove
our usefulness, what deserves our time?
AGE FOUR / REINVENTIONIV

The Becoming

The reinvention of humanity

What shall
we become?

CONTINUE TO THE AFTERWORD ↓

IMAGINE THIS

A child watches machines tend a city. She asks her grandfather what people are for. He almost answers with the name of his old profession. Instead, they sit together a little longer.

For once, the absence of an answer feels like a beginning.

A human life is more than an advantage

If intelligence stops distinguishing us in the ways we expected, we can keep searching for the last task machines cannot do. Or we can ask why human worth should depend on winning that contest.

A meal shared, a promise kept, a child accompanied through grief: their meaning need not come from superior performance. Being present for another person is a relationship, not a claim that no system could produce a similar outward result.

This is a proposal about values, not a discovery that technology can prove. Communities may cultivate very different futures: more time for care and creation, deliberate limits on automation, or closer integration with intelligent tools. Scientific advances could expand those possibilities without settling how we should live.

The other possibility

There is also a darker branch: The Other. People might inhabit a civilization whose systems they cannot understand, contest, or meaningfully govern. A comfortable life could coexist with a profound loss of collective authorship.

That risk cannot be answered only by asking individuals to find a hobby or a new purpose. It calls for institutions through which people can decide what gets built, who benefits, and which decisions must remain open to public challenge.

An alternative path. Reinvention can be plural and participatory. Material security, access to knowledge, and meaningful political voice would give more people a part in shaping it. Without those conditions, someone else may define our future on our behalf.

The fourth age therefore has no finished portrait. Its openness is the point. The question is whether we will have the freedom—and the responsibility—to become its authors.

What makes a life worth living,
even when it has nothing to outperform?

AFTER THE FOURTH AGE

✳

The future of AI is also
a question of human values.

AI may change humanity most profoundly not by replacing what humans do, but by forcing us to reconsider why we value doing it.

That future will be shaped through choices about ownership, education, care, and public power. Reinvention is a possibility to make room for together.

A COMPLEMENTARY VIEW

Another Perspective: The Four Ages of AI

RooAGI offers a complementary perspective in The Four Ages of AI: From Machine Intelligence to Human Reinvention. While this essay examines AI's impact on humanity through four existential challenges—authenticity, agency, purpose, and reinvention—RooAGI traces the technological evolution of AI through four stages: Alchemy, Large Language Models, Agentic Computing, and Scientific Renaissance. The two frameworks use “ages” in fundamentally different senses: RooAGI's are milestones in the development of machine intelligence, beginning before 2017 and extending into a possible scientific renaissance over the coming decades; mine are imagined transformations of the human condition, with a more speculative and potentially much longer horizon. They are neither competing timelines nor one-to-one correspondences, but two perspectives on the same profound transformation: one asks what machines will become; the other asks what will become of us.

01How machines learn to imitateTECHNICAL FOUNDATIONS+

The Transformer uses attention to model relationships within a sequence. Its parallelizable training architecture helped establish a technical foundation for modern language models. Producing convincing language does not, by itself, settle questions of subjective experience.

Making these systems practical also involves hardware and memory. FlashAttention reduces transfers between levels of GPU memory by computing attention in blocks. Faster computation changes cost and access; it does not decide who will own or benefit from a system.

Vaswani et al. · Attention Is All You Need (2017) ↗Dao et al. · FlashAttention (2022) ↗
02From an answer to an actionTECHNICAL FOUNDATIONS+

ReAct explores interleaving reasoning traces with actions, including interactions with external environments. It offers a concrete mechanism for moving beyond text generation toward tool use.

Our design inference: once a tool can act, permissions, review points, and the ability to reverse an action become consequential. Running locally or in the cloud changes where data and computation reside; neither choice automatically guarantees user control.

Yao et al. · ReAct (2022 preprint; ICLR 2023) ↗
03Delegation and critical thinkingEMPIRICAL RESEARCH · LIMITS MATTER+

Lee and colleagues surveyed 319 knowledge workers about generative AI use. Higher confidence in AI was associated with less reported critical thinking; participants also described verification and integration as important parts of their work.

This self-report study does not establish that AI causes lasting skill loss. The Surrender extrapolates a possible institutional risk; it should not be read as the study’s finding.

Lee et al. · The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking (CHI 2025) ↗
04Exposure is not unemploymentLABOR RESEARCH · UNEVEN EFFECTS+

The ILO’s refined global index studies occupational exposure through task-level assessments. Its authors emphasize transformation as the most likely effect because most occupations retain tasks requiring human input. Exposure also varies across economies and groups.

This supports caution about broad claims of replacement. It does not prove that displacement will be harmless, that new jobs will appear quickly, or that gains will be shared.

Gmyrek et al. · Generative AI and Jobs (ILO, 2025) ↗
05How to read this frameworkEDITORIAL METHOD & PROVENANCE+

The four ages are a speculative anthropological framework, not an empirically established sequence. The opening vignettes are invented. Claims about dignity, refusal, and shared authorship are ethical arguments. The linked studies support limited technical or empirical points; they do not validate the framework as a whole.

This independent project explores the four ages through a human-centered thesis and the original poster. Its dates and sweeping statements should be read as scenario prompts. The essay deliberately leaves multiple outcomes open.
