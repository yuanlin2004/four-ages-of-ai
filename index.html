THE PREMISEAn essay on human reinvention

We taught machines to think.
Now, what will we make
of ourselves?

Modern society rewards people for what they know and what they can do. Schools develop those abilities. Professions pay for them. Expertise gives people authority.

What happens when machines can do more of that work? The question reaches beyond jobs. It touches the letters we write, the choices we make, the respect we seek, and our sense of what makes a life worthwhile.

This essay proposes four ages through which to examine that change. Each asks what happens to a different human claim: this is my voice; this is my choice; this is my contribution; this is who I am.

Each age also has a counterpart in Black Mirror, Charlie Brooker’s anthology series, named for the dark glass of a switched-off screen. Its episodes tend to begin with a feature someone wanted: a rating, a perfect match, a voice that comes back. The trouble arrives later, once everyone depends on it. Watching that unfold lets us rehearse a future before we have to live in it. [7]

A second fiction looks from the other side. Detroit: Become Human, a 2018 video game, follows three androids through a near-future city and lets the player decide what they do. After each chapter, a flowchart shows the branch you took and the branches you left behind. Each age here closes with a flowchart of its own, because none of them is settled yet. [7]

↳

Four lenses, not a timetable. These ages may overlap, unfold unevenly, or never arrive as imagined. The opening scenes are fiction. The argument is an invitation to debate, not a prediction of a single human future.
