AN ESSAY · AI AND MATHEMATICS

Letting go of the problems

Terence Tao’s worry, read from the world outside the department.

In early October 2026, OpenAI released a large batch of preprints claiming solutions to open problems in mathematics. Counts differ from source to source. [2] Terence Tao, a Fields Medallist and one of the field’s public voices on AI, wrote that he was “horrified by the many person-years of ongoing patient and deliberately slow research efforts” that such releases disrupt. [2] According to The New York Times, he called the coming results “toxic waste” before the release and “maybe treated waste” after it. [3]

What Tao gets right

Tao is no opponent of these tools. He uses them, and for several years he has written carefully about how to use them well: check any output you depend on, keep some friction in the work, disclose what a machine did. [1] In a lecture at Caltech on 9 October he argued that solving problems was always a proxy for understanding, and that “further blind optimization of problem-solving alone is now actively harmful to the long-term health of mathematics.” [4] A September declaration he signed with other Fields Medallists makes the same case. [5]

He has also asked mathematicians to “organise, become activists, get a little political,” so that a technology company does not settle these questions for them. [6] This essay’s fourth age ends on the same branch: decide together, in public. And the grief is real. A graduate student who spent four years on a problem and woke to find it answered in a preprint has lost something, and deserves more than a shrug.

Where the worry stops at the department door

The same lecture contains a thought experiment. An AI is asked to find a cure for cancer that passes a stage 3 clinical trial. It produces a cocktail of new chemicals; nobody knows how it was found, and it passes the trial. The slide asks: “Could the AI solution be somehow misaligned by exploiting a weakness in the trial process or its math models?” [4]

The oncologist Vinay Prasad answered on 10 October. Medicine, he wrote, already relies on drugs whose mechanisms are partly unknown, and on anaesthetics no one can fully explain. A positive phase 3 trial is the evidence patients and doctors act on. If a machine produced such a cure, “we will be on our knees thanking it.” [7] On Hacker News the argument split along the same line. Some defended mechanistic understanding as the heart of science; others wrote that “Tao is a mathematician discussing complex fields which aren’t math.” [8]

Prasad’s point lands. The narrow worry on the slide, that an optimizer might game a trial, is fair, and the remedy for it is a better trial. For someone with cancer, a treatment that works is the goal. Understanding may follow, and it is welcome when it does. Much of ordinary life already runs on things that work before anyone can say exactly why.

Other people got here first

Tao mourns patient, slow research overtaken by a machine. Translators, illustrators, voice actors, junior programmers and call-centre staff met the same machine earlier. Few of them had tenure, an institute to host a workshop, or twenty-five Fields Medallists to sign a declaration. Many simply found that the work they had trained for could be bought by the minute. This essay’s third age, The Irrelevance, was written with them in mind.

Research that is “deliberately slow” is a privilege most working people never had. The mathematicians’ loss is still real. It is also one version of a common loss, and what others have learned from theirs applies here: a role can end without the person losing their worth.

What letting go would look like

Tao has named what is ending. On Numberphile on 15 October he called it “the end of the romantic era of mathematics, the age of heroes.” [11] The hero is the lone mind who spends years on one problem and cracks it, and that figure is what has to be let go. He has already let go of part of the picture: Math 2.0, as he describes it, stops treating solved problems as the measure of the field. The next step is smaller than it sounds.

He calls open problems “something resembling a non-renewable resource,” since once a machine solves one it cannot be unsolved. [9] From outside the department, an open problem looks like a question humanity asked and a field held in trust. When it is answered, the answer goes out to engineers, physicists, and whoever builds on it next. A reserve that is finally spent has done its job.

Tao told The Atlantic that AI tools “are like taking a helicopter to drop you off at the site,” skipping the hike where insight forms. [1] People still climb mountains that helicopters can reach, and the climb keeps its worth for the climber. That worth cannot depend on grounding the helicopter. Mathematics can keep the climb, for students, for anyone who wants to understand, and for its own sake. It is the second branch of the third age: paint something nobody will buy.

Understanding is valuable to whoever gains it. It does not need to stand at the gate as a toll every result must pay before the world may use it. The “talk test” for publication in his ICM essay, that authors should be able to give a clear expert talk on their results, is a sensible rule for journals. [1] It should stay a rule for journals.

What to hold on to

Some things should not be let go. Who owns the systems that now produce proofs, who can afford to run them, and whether a student in a poorer country can use them are fair questions; a press report says they were raised at a UCLA workshop this month. [10] So were credit and training for the people coming next. Tao’s call to organise fits here. Pointed at access, credit and teaching, it helps everyone. Pointed at holding answers back until humans have digested them, it asks the rest of the world to wait for mathematics.

The answer mathematics already has

The afterword of this essay asks why we still value doing what machines can do. Mathematicians have an answer ready: doing it is how a person comes to understand. That answer holds outside the department, for the climber, the painter and the patient alike, and letting go of the problems leaves it intact.
