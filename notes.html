01How machines learn to imitateTECHNICAL FOUNDATIONS+

The Transformer uses attention to model relationships within a sequence. Its parallelizable training architecture helped establish a technical foundation for modern language models. Producing convincing language does not, by itself, settle questions of subjective experience.

Making these systems practical also involves hardware and memory. FlashAttention reduces transfers between levels of GPU memory by computing attention in blocks. Faster computation changes cost and access; it does not decide who will own or benefit from a system.

Whether fluent language implies experience remains open. A 2023 review by Butlin, Long, and colleagues derives indicator properties from scientific theories of consciousness and concludes that no current system is a strong candidate, while finding no obvious technical barrier to building one.

Vaswani et al. · Attention Is All You Need (2017) ↗Dao et al. · FlashAttention (2022) ↗Butlin, Long et al. · Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence (2023) ↗
02From an answer to an actionTECHNICAL FOUNDATIONS+

ReAct explores interleaving reasoning traces with actions, including interactions with external environments. It offers a concrete mechanism for moving beyond text generation toward tool use.

Our design inference: once a tool can act, permissions, review points, and the ability to reverse an action become consequential. Running locally or in the cloud changes where data and computation reside; neither choice automatically guarantees user control.

Chan and colleagues propose agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, and activity logs so that the actions of deployed agents can be seen and audited. Bainbridge’s much older “Ironies of Automation” warns that operators who only supervise lose the practice they need when the system fails, a pattern now discussed for AI assistants.

Yao et al. · ReAct (2022 preprint; ICLR 2023) ↗Chan et al. · Visibility into AI Agents (FAccT 2024) ↗Bainbridge · Ironies of Automation (Automatica, 1983) ↗
03Delegation and critical thinkingEMPIRICAL RESEARCH · LIMITS MATTER+

Lee and colleagues surveyed 319 knowledge workers about generative AI use. Higher confidence in AI was associated with less reported critical thinking; participants also described verification and integration as important parts of their work.

This self-report study does not establish that AI causes lasting skill loss. The Surrender extrapolates a possible institutional risk; it should not be read as the study’s finding.

A field experiment by Bastani and colleagues with nearly a thousand high school students found that unrestricted GPT-4 access improved practice scores but lowered exam performance once access was removed. A tutor version designed to give hints rather than answers largely avoided that harm.

Lee et al. · The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking (CHI 2025) ↗Bastani et al. · Generative AI Without Guardrails Can Harm Learning (PNAS, 2025) ↗
04Exposure is not unemploymentLABOR RESEARCH · UNEVEN EFFECTS+

The ILO’s refined global index studies occupational exposure through task-level assessments. Its authors emphasize transformation as the most likely effect because most occupations retain tasks requiring human input. Exposure also varies across economies and groups.

This supports caution about broad claims of replacement. It does not prove that displacement will be harmless, that new jobs will appear quickly, or that gains will be shared.

Early experiments point to uneven gains. Writing tasks were completed faster in Noy and Zhang’s experiment, and a customer support assistant raised productivity most for less experienced agents in Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond’s field study. Eloundou and colleagues estimate which tasks are exposed, not which jobs disappear.

Income is not the only loss. Paul and Moser’s meta-analysis found that unemployment is associated with worse mental health, and that re-employment is associated with recovery.

Gmyrek et al. · Generative AI and Jobs (ILO, 2025) ↗Eloundou et al. · GPTs are GPTs (Science, 2024) ↗Noy & Zhang · Productivity Effects of Generative AI (Science, 2023) ↗Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond · Generative AI at Work (QJE, 2025) ↗Paul & Moser · Unemployment Impairs Mental Health (2009) ↗
05When imitation meets judgmentEMPIRICAL RESEARCH · AUTHENTICITY+

In experiments by Jakesch, Hancock, and Naaman, people could not reliably tell AI-generated self-presentations from human ones. They relied on cues such as first-person pronouns and family topics, which the systems could reproduce.

Writing with a model can also shape what we say. In a further study, people who co-wrote with an opinionated model shifted their stated views toward it. Doshi and Hauser found that AI ideas made individual stories more creative but made the stories as a group more similar to one another.

Content Credentials, defined by the C2PA standard, attach signed records of how a piece of media was made and edited. They can show provenance; they cannot show sincerity.

Jakesch et al. · Human Heuristics for AI-Generated Language Are Flawed (PNAS, 2023) ↗Jakesch et al. · Co-Writing with Opinionated Language Models (CHI 2023) ↗Doshi & Hauser · Generative AI and Creative Diversity (Science Advances, 2024) ↗C2PA · Content Credentials Technical Specification ↗
06Comfort without controlARGUMENT & HISTORY · NOT A FORECAST+

Kulveit and colleagues argue that AI could weaken human influence without any sudden takeover. As economies, states, and cultures rely less on human labor and participation, the pressures that keep institutions responsive to people may fade. This is a position paper, not an empirical finding.

The question of free time is older than AI. In 1930 Keynes imagined grandchildren freed from economic necessity and facing “the permanent problem” of how to live wisely and well.

Kulveit et al. · Gradual Disempowerment (ICML 2025 position) ↗Keynes · Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren (1930) ↗
07Fiction as rehearsalBLACK MIRROR · FICTION+

Black Mirror is fiction. The essay uses its episodes as thought experiments that illustrate patterns; they prove nothing on their own. They exaggerate to make a pattern visible, and the pattern borrowed here is the slow one: harm that builds through convenience, defaults, and consent forms, with no single moment of takeover.

Introducing the series in 2011, Charlie Brooker wrote that the black mirror of the title is the one found on every wall, on every desk, and in the palm of every hand: the screen of a TV, a monitor, or a smartphone.

Episodes referenced: Fifteen Million Merits (2011), Be Right Back (2013), Nosedive (2016), San Junipero (2016), Hang the DJ (2017), Joan Is Awful (2023), and Common People (2025).

Brooker · The dark side of our gadget addiction (The Guardian, 2011) ↗
08How to read this frameworkEDITORIAL METHOD & PROVENANCE+

The four ages are a speculative anthropological framework, not an empirically established sequence. The opening vignettes are invented. Claims about dignity, refusal, and shared authorship are ethical arguments. The linked studies support limited technical or empirical points; they do not validate the framework as a whole.

This independent project explores the four ages through a human-centered thesis and the original poster. Its dates and sweeping statements should be read as scenario prompts. The essay deliberately leaves multiple outcomes open.
