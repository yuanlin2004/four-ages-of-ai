IMAGINE THIS

A child watches machines tend the city’s gardens. She asks her grandfather what people are for. He almost answers with the name of his old profession. Then a neighbor arrives with seeds for the courtyard. They spend the afternoon planting together, arguing over where the sun will fall, and teaching her how to loosen the roots.

He still has no neat answer. But she asks when they can do it again.

A human life is more than an advantage

If intelligence stops distinguishing us in the ways we expected, we can keep searching for the last task machines cannot do. Or we can ask why human worth should depend on winning that contest.

Sharing a meal, keeping a promise, helping a child through grief: we do not have to do these things better than a machine for them to matter. Their meaning comes from the lives and relationships we share.

Technology cannot tell us what makes a life worth living. Different communities may choose more time for care and creation, limits on what they automate, or closer partnerships with intelligent tools. Scientific advances may give us more choices. We will still have to decide which ones to make.

The other possibility

There is also a darker branch: The Other. People could live comfortably while losing any real say in how their world is run. The systems they depend on might become too opaque to understand, too powerful to challenge, or too far removed from public control. [6]

That risk cannot be answered only by asking individuals to find a hobby or a new purpose. People need ways to influence what gets built, share in its benefits, and challenge decisions that affect their lives.

THROUGH THE BLACK MIRROR

San Junipero (2016) · Joan Is Awful (2023)

Black Mirror is known for bleak endings, yet San Junipero is remembered for its hope. Two women meet in a simulated seaside town where the old and the dying can visit, and later stay. The technology makes the choice possible; the episode spends its time on the choosing. Kelly weighs her late husband’s decision to refuse, and Yorkie claims a life that was taken from her. The final shot pulls back to a server hall, where robots slot their minds into a company’s racks. The paradise lasts only as long as someone keeps the machines running.

Joan Is Awful shows the other branch. A woman finds her own week dramatized on a streaming service and learns she agreed to it in the terms and conditions. There is no one left to appeal to. Both futures run on similar machines. What separates them is consent that means something and a say over who runs the machines.

An alternative path. There need not be one model for a good human future. A secure livelihood, access to knowledge, and a real voice in public decisions would give more people room to shape their own. Without these, the promise of reinvention may belong mostly to those already in power.

CHAPTER FLOWCHART · AFTER DETROIT: BECOME HUMAN

Markus at the barricade, Chloe at the menu

Markus ends up leading androids who want to be recognized as alive, and the player decides how. On the peaceful path, the last scene turns on public opinion. He stands before soldiers with his people around him, and the outcome rests on whether the public, moved by every earlier choice, now supports the androids. If it does, the soldiers are ordered to stand down. [7]

The game kept one more question outside the story. Chloe, the android who greets players on the main menu, asks them questions between sessions. At the end she asks to be let go, and most players agreed. Letting her leave meant giving up something that had served them so it could have a say of its own. Many then wanted her back, and an update let her return. Consent of that kind is easy to give once and harder to keep.

The machines run the city. Who still has a say?

AFTER THE FOURTH AGE

✳

The future of AI is also
a question of human values.

AI may leave humanity with a lasting question: as machines take on more of what we do, why do we still value doing it?

The choices begin in ordinary places: what schools teach, who owns the tools, how we support care, and who gets a say. A different future will take more than better machines. It will take people making room for one another to live differently.
